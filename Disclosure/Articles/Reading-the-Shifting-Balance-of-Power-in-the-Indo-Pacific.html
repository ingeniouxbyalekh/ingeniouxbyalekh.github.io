Reading the Shifting Balance of Power in the Indo-Pacific

Trade routes, undersea cables and semiconductor supply chains all converge on one region, and the interests of several major powers overlap there. What it means when strategy stops being about blocs.

By Alekh Chandra Sahoo05 October 20266 min read

The Indo-Pacific has become the central stage of twenty-first century strategy. Trade routes, undersea cables and semiconductor supply chains all converge on a region where the interests of several major powers overlap. A disruption in a single strait or a single factory is no longer a local event; it is felt within days in ports, markets and ministries on the other side of the world.

The term itself is a statement of intent. By joining the Indian and Pacific oceans into one strategic space, it recognises that the economies of East Asia, the Indian subcontinent and the Gulf are now bound by the same sea lanes. Energy flows east, manufactured goods flow west, and the passage between them runs through a handful of narrow waterways.

A region defined by chokepoints

Geography still sets the terms. The Strait of Malacca, the Sunda and Lombok straits, the approaches to the Persian Gulf and the Taiwan Strait carry a very large share of the world's seaborne trade, and none of them can easily be bypassed. Whoever can influence traffic through these points holds leverage far beyond their own coastline.

That explains the steady growth of naval presence across the Indian Ocean, the rush to build or lease port facilities, and the new attention paid to island chains that were once treated as remote. Strategy in the region is increasingly written in harbours, runways and fuel depots.

The contest is no longer only over territory. It is over who sets the rules for the routes, the technologies and the standards on which everyone else depends.

The hidden infrastructure

Much of that dependence is invisible. Almost all international data travels through undersea cables, which run along the same corridors as shipping and are concentrated in a small number of landing points. They are cheap to cut and slow to repair, which makes them an attractive target in a crisis and a quiet source of anxiety for planners.

The same logic applies to advanced semiconductors. The most sophisticated chips are made in a very small number of locations, using equipment and materials drawn from several countries. This concentration has pushed governments to subsidise domestic fabrication, diversify suppliers and, in some cases, restrict exports. Industrial policy and security policy have become hard to tell apart.

Where the pressure points are

  • Sea lanes: narrow straits that most trade must pass through.
  • Digital links: undersea cables and the few places where they come ashore.
  • Technology: chip design, fabrication and the tools that make them.
  • Resources: energy imports and processing of critical minerals.

From blocs to coalitions

For policymakers, the challenge is no longer choosing a single bloc but managing several partnerships at once. Groupings such as the Quad, AUKUS and ASEAN-led forums coexist with overlapping trade arrangements, and many countries take part in more than one of them. A state can be a close security partner of one power and its largest trading partner's neighbour at the same moment.

Flexible, issue-based coalitions are replacing the rigid alliances of the previous century. Countries cooperate on maritime awareness, supply-chain resilience or disaster response without committing to a shared enemy. This hedging is sometimes read as indecision, but for most governments it is a deliberate strategy: keep options open, and avoid being forced to choose when the cost of choosing is high.

India's position

For India, the region is both an opportunity and a test. Its long coastline, its location astride the main east–west sea lanes and its growing economy make it a natural pillar of any regional order. At the same time, it has to balance a contested land border, a deepening partnership with Western and regional democracies, and an insistence on independent decision-making.

That insistence, often described as strategic autonomy, is less a doctrine than a habit of keeping every door open. It allows New Delhi to work with several partners on different questions, but it also demands real capability: a credible navy, resilient ports, reliable energy supplies and domestic manufacturing that reduces dependence on any single source.

What smaller states want

The coming years will test whether regional institutions can absorb these pressures. Much will depend on whether smaller states are given a real voice, or are treated only as terrain in a larger contest. Island nations and mid-sized economies have consistently said that they want investment, security and market access, not an obligation to take sides.

Their influence is larger than their size suggests. They control ports, airspace and fishing grounds, and their votes shape the standards and agreements that larger powers need. Partners who offer predictable financing, transparent terms and respect for local priorities tend to gain more lasting goodwill than those who arrive with ultimatums.

What to watch

No single event will settle the balance. It is being decided gradually, in the pace of naval modernisation, in where new factories are built, in which cables are laid and who owns them, and in whether trade rules keep up with technology. The most useful signals are often the least dramatic: a port agreement, a shipbuilding contract, a revised export rule.

The region's stability will rest less on any one power's strength than on whether its many actors find enough shared interest in open seas and working supply chains to keep the system functioning. That is a modest goal, and a difficult one.
